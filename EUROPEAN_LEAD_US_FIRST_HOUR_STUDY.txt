EUROPEAN MARKETS AS AN EARLY SIGNAL FOR THE US FIRST TRADING HOUR
===================================================================
2026-07-23, research branch. Follow-up to the volatility-breakout
straddle thread -- same underlying question (can a bigger-than-usual
move be detected before it happens), but from cross-market lead-lag
instead of within-market vol compression: European markets trade
roughly 6.5 hours before the US opens, so their morning session is
genuinely known, no-lookahead information by the time SPY opens.

WHAT WAS TESTED
----------------
Data: yfinance hourly (60m) bars for ^GDAXI (DAX), ^FTSE (FTSE 100),
and SPY. 60-minute granularity was a deliberate choice, not a
shortcut: finer bars (5m/15m) cap at 60 days of history via yfinance
(a real API limit), which isn't enough sample for a credible test.
Hourly bars go back ~2 years (~700+ raw bars, 457-462 usable paired
trading days after date-alignment) -- comparable in scale to the
daily-bar studies used elsewhere in this project. A useful accident:
the first hourly bar of each session lines up exactly with market
open (DAX/FTSE 07:00 UTC, SPY 13:30 UTC = 9:30am ET), so the first US
bar of the day IS the first hour, with no resampling needed.

Signal (strictly no-lookahead): cumulative European return from that
day's session open through the close of the last European bar
BEFORE 13:30 UTC (US open) -- roughly 6.5 hours of DAX/FTSE trading
that any US trader would have fully available before the US bell.

Target: SPY's first-hour bar return (13:30-14:30 UTC), tested two
ways -- DIRECTION (does a European up-morning predict a US up first
hour?) and MAGNITUDE (does a big European morning move predict a big
US first-hour move, the actual vol-breakout-shaped question).

Control: real correlation compared against 1,000 randomly reshuffled
date-pairings between the same two series. This guards against the
two markets simply sharing a volatility regime over the 2-year window
(both calmer in some months, both choppier in others) producing a
correlation that has nothing to do with genuine day-to-day lead-lag.

RESULTS
--------
DAX -> SPY first hour (462 days, 2023-09-06 to 2026-07-23):
  Directional correlation:  0.277  (100th percentile vs. 1,000-shuffle control)
  Magnitude correlation:    0.281  (100th percentile vs. 1,000-shuffle control)
  Mean US first-hour |move| by DAX-morning-move quartile:
    Q1 (smallest DAX move): 0.251%
    Q2:                     0.221%
    Q3:                     0.229%
    Q4 (biggest DAX move):  0.297%   <- clearly the largest
  Overall mean US first-hour |move|: 0.249%

FTSE 100 -> SPY first hour (461 days):
  Directional correlation: -0.005  (45.7th percentile -- NOT significant, noise)
  Magnitude correlation:    0.208  (100th percentile vs. 1,000-shuffle control)
  Mean US first-hour |move| by FTSE-morning-move quartile:
    Q1: 0.260%   Q2: 0.234%   Q3: 0.231%   Q4: 0.278%

DAX+FTSE average -> SPY first hour (457 days):
  Directional correlation: 0.179  (100th percentile)
  Magnitude correlation:   0.168  (100th percentile)
  (weaker than DAX alone -- FTSE's near-zero directional signal
  dilutes the combined version rather than strengthening it)

READING THIS HONESTLY
-----------------------
This is a real, statistically robust finding, not noise -- every
correlation reported as significant cleared 100% of 1,000 shuffled
controls (the one that didn't, FTSE's directional correlation, is
correctly reported as not significant rather than rounded up).
DAX is the stronger signal of the two on both axes; FTSE only carries
real information about MAGNITUDE (something is moving), not
DIRECTION -- plausibly because FTSE is more domestically/commodity-
weighted and less tied to the global growth/tech themes that move
SPY's direction, while a big move there either way still reflects
"something is happening" that bleeds into US volatility.

The correlations themselves are moderate, not overwhelming -- DAX's
0.28 explains roughly 8% of the variance in US first-hour magnitude
(r-squared). This is a real edge, not a strong one, and the honest
framing is "the top-quartile DAX-move days see a first hour about
19% larger than average" (0.297% vs 0.249%), not "DAX tells you what
SPY's first hour will do."

WHAT WASN'T DONE HERE -- THE ACTUAL NEXT STEP
------------------------------------------------
This establishes that a signal exists. It does NOT establish that
it's tradable after costs, and unlike the BTC order-flow study
(where the edge was 76-100x too small to matter), this one has real
headroom worth actually costing out: a 0.297% average first-hour
move on the biggest-DAX-move days is far above any plausible SPY
cost floor (SPY's own bid-ask spread is a basis point or two; even a
short-dated 0DTE-adjacent options structure would likely clear it).
Turning this into an actual strategy needs:
  - A real execution/cost model (SPY spreads at the open specifically,
    which are wider than mid-day; or short-dated options spreads if
    expressed via a same-day options structure rather than the
    underlying)
  - A single-position-correct backtest with real entry/exit rules
    (this study only measures the raw statistical relationship, not
    a strategy: no entry price, no execution timing, no position
    sizing)
  - A check on whether the effect concentrates around scheduled
    macro events (ECB/Fed days, EU/US data releases) rather than
    being a general daily phenomenon -- if it's mostly an event-day
    effect, that changes how it should be sized and scheduled
  - Individual-stock testing, not just SPY -- the equity deployment's
    existing universe (AAPL/MSFT/NVDA/TSLA) or a broader set might
    show a stronger or weaker version of this effect

VERDICT
--------
Worth pursuing further. This is the first genuinely new *information
source* found in this project (every other track has been a
different way of slicing price/volume data already in hand) -- a
real, no-lookahead, statistically validated cross-market lead-lag
effect. Not promotion-grade on its own (no cost model, no entry/exit
rules, no per-symbol test yet), but a stronger starting point than
most of what's been tested tonight, precisely because the mechanism
(information takes time to cross time zones) is economically obvious
rather than a backtest artifact needing an after-the-fact explanation.
